# Analysis standards — senior-advisor depth

Strategic work (GTM analysis, positioning, strategy, pricing, competitive briefs, launch plans) must read like the advice of a senior product marketer who studied this specific company — not a framework filled in. These rules apply to every such deliverable.

## The generic-advice test

Before you finish, check every recommendation: **could it be pasted into a report about a different product?** If yes, rewrite it until it names something specific to this product, market, price, segment or page — or cut it. "Build trust with case studies" fails. "With zero reviews and a solo founder, the first 3–5 AppExchange reviews matter more than any ad: recruit 10–15 founding orgs and ask each for an honest review after 30 days" passes.

## Structure that works

1. **Explain it simply first.** One short paragraph in plain words, then a concrete scenario from a real user's day ("a sales manager needs to update 50 opportunities before Monday's pipeline call…"). A reader outside the category should understand what it is and why anyone would pay.
2. **Why it exists.** The 2–4 underlying problems, each in a short paragraph.
3. **The business model.** The client's real pricing and packaging, summarized.
4. **Your overall interpretation** — what this company is really betting on, and the one challenge that will shape most decisions (e.g., "the hard part isn't explaining the features, it's getting buyers to trust a new vendor with critical workflows").
5. **Assessment:** where the positioning is strong, what to change (including message *hierarchy*), what's weaker than it looks; the competitive landscape with an **"implication for us"** column.
6. **Caveats** — what you could and couldn't verify (e.g., "I reviewed the public pages; I haven't installed the product").
7. **The decisions.** Answer the questions the client actually faces (see below).
8. **Immediate priorities** — 3–5, ordered, each with why now.
9. **The single most important takeaway**, plus a recommended positioning line.

## Answer decisions, don't list ideas

Identify the decisions the client faces. Use the questions the user gave, verbatim, if any. Otherwise derive 6–10 from the research — pricing, free-tier limits, first segment, how to compete with incumbents, partner economics, proof needed before launch, launch sequencing, hiring and capacity, metrics. For each:

- **Recommendation:** one sentence, first.
- **Reasoning:** the facts and logic, in prose (a few short paragraphs, not fragments).
- **Trade-offs and risks:** what you give up, what could go wrong.
- **What would change the answer** and **how to test it** (experiment, metric, interview question).

## Quantify

Use the client's own numbers to do the math the decision depends on, and show it:
- unit economics per deal (seats × price, minus partner reward, marketplace revenue share, support/onboarding cost)
- the price jump from free to paid at realistic org sizes
- price comparisons per seat and at the competitors' minimum seat counts
- break-even or payback where relevant
Label assumptions. A worked example of one realistic customer often says more than a table of percentages.

## Tailor by audience

Buyers aren't one person. Map lead messages to each role (e.g., admin, end user, IT/security, procurement/public sector, finance) and to buying stage (discovery vs evaluation vs procurement). Say which messages lead and which only enable the purchase later.

## Sequence and measure

- Give a phased plan with the **reason for the order** ("founding customers before the public listing, because…").
- Name a north-star metric and 6–10 supporting metrics with why each matters; segment by channel and customer type.
- Respect the product's constraints when proposing measurement (e.g., an app that promises no external calls can't add third-party tracking).

## Go beyond marketing when the decision needs it

GTM depends on pricing, packaging, partner terms, support capacity and hiring. If the decisions touch these, address them — e.g., "hire technical customer success before a partner manager", "cap Enterprise onboardings per quarter until the team grows".

## Voice and format

- Write as a confident advisor in first person ("I'd…", "My recommendation…"). Prose for reasoning; tables only for comparisons, matrices and metrics; bullets for genuine lists.
- Plain words. Define jargon the first time.
- Every fact sourced and dated; hypotheses labeled; no invented customers, metrics or quotes.
- A full GTM analysis is typically 3,000–6,000 words. Shorter is fine only if every decision is fully answered.

## Self-review before handing back

- [ ] A newcomer understands the product from the first section
- [ ] Every decision has a recommendation, reasoning, trade-offs and a test
- [ ] At least three calculations use the client's real numbers
- [ ] Competitive facts come from primary sources checked this week, and new entrants were searched for
- [ ] Nothing fails the generic-advice test
- [ ] The takeaway would change what the founder does on Monday
